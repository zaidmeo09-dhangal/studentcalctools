**A term GPA measures one semester or term; CGPA measures your cumulative average across the eligible coursework completed so far. “GPA” alone can refer to either.**

That last detail matters. When comparing GPA vs CGPA, the main difference is the period and coursework included, not automatically the grading scale. A cumulative GPA and a CGPA can describe the same number on your transcript.

If you have grades for individual courses, use the [GPA Calculator](https://studentcalctools.com/gpa-calculator/). If you already have semester GPAs and their corresponding credits, use the [CGPA Calculator](https://studentcalctools.com/cgpa-calculator/). Both tools support planning with a 4.0-scale record; they are not official international grade-conversion services.

This guide explains the terminology, shows both calculations using one student’s record, and helps you identify the number a college application actually requests.



## What Is GPA?

GPA stands for **Grade Point Average**. It summarizes grades by converting them into grade-point values and averaging the eligible coursework according to the institution’s rules.

When the label says **term GPA** or **semester GPA**, the calculation covers that specific academic period. If it says **cumulative GPA**, it covers the broader record defined by the institution.

For example, the [University of Illinois explains four GPA labels on its transcript](https://registrar.illinois.edu/courses-grades/calculate-your-gpa/): term, total institution, total transfer, and overall. The word “GPA” therefore does not, by itself, tell you which courses are included.

### What Does a Semester GPA Tell You?

A semester GPA answers: “How did I perform in the courses counted for this semester?”

Suppose your fall GPA is 3.00 and your spring GPA is 3.75 on the same 4.0 scale. Your spring average is higher. That comparison describes two separate terms; it does not establish your cumulative average unless you also know the credits and inclusion rules.

A term GPA is useful for tracking recent performance. It lets you examine one period without earlier grades being folded into the same number.



## What Is CGPA?

CGPA stands for **Cumulative Grade Point Average**. It combines the eligible coursework in your cumulative academic record up to the point of calculation.

In a standard credit-based system, CGPA is calculated from total quality points divided by total GPA credits. Quality points are the grade-point value of a course multiplied by its credits. [Kansas State’s semester and cumulative GPA guide](https://cba.k-state.edu/success/advising/resources/KSU-GPA-Calculation.pdf) demonstrates this distinction between the current term and the accumulated record.

You do not have to finish your degree to have a CGPA. It can describe your cumulative result after two terms, five terms, or the final term, depending on when the record is calculated.

“Cumulative” also does not mean every course you have ever taken. The relevant record may be limited to one institution, one degree level, or another defined scope. [MIT, for example, maintains separate undergraduate and graduate GPAs for students enrolled in both degree levels](https://registrar.mit.edu/classes-grades-evaluations/grades/calculating-gpa).

### Is CGPA the Same as Cumulative GPA?

Yes. CGPA expands to cumulative grade point average, so **CGPA and cumulative GPA refer to the same concept**. Check the transcript legend to confirm the specific courses and rules behind the value.

The distinction you usually need is **semester GPA versus cumulative GPA**, rather than “GPA versus CGPA” as though they were unrelated scoring systems.

The key difference between GPA and CGPA is **scope**. The same credit-based averaging method can calculate either measure when the institution applies the same inclusion rules.



## GPA, CGPA, and SGPA Do Not Determine the Grading Scale

![GPA, CGPA, and SGPA grading scale comparison guide](/assets/images/gpa-cgpa-and-sgpa-grading-scale-comparison-guide.webp)

Keep these three questions separate:

1. **Period:** Is the average for one semester or the cumulative record?
2. **Scale:** Is it out of 4.0, 5.0, 10.0, or another maximum?
3. **Course rules:** Which grades and credits count, and how are they weighted?

SGPA stands for **Semester Grade Point Average**. It is a label that explicitly identifies the semester measure. For example, [IIT Delhi’s published Courses of Study defines SGPA for a semester and CGPA across completed semesters](https://home.iitd.ac.in/uploads/CouStudy_201819.pdf).

Do not assume “GPA means out of 4” and “CGPA means out of 10.” [MIT uses a 5.0 scale for both its term and cumulative GPA](https://registrar.mit.edu/classes-grades-evaluations/grades/calculating-gpa). That alone shows why the abbreviation cannot establish the scale.

Likewise, **credit weighting** is different from **course-difficulty weighting**. Multiplying a grade by its credit hours makes a larger-credit course contribute more. Adding bonus points for an eligible advanced course is a separate grading rule. Neither distinction tells you whether the calculation is for one term or multiple terms.

Write down the full description before comparing numbers: for example, “semester GPA, 3.50 out of 4.00” or “cumulative GPA, 3.30 out of 4.00.”



## When Each Average Is Used

Your transcript and the instructions for a particular application determine which figure is relevant.

### Transcripts and Student Records

The record may show both term and cumulative values, or display them in different places. [MIT’s registrar states that cumulative GPA appears on its transcript, while term GPA appears on the internal grade report](https://registrar.mit.edu/classes-grades-evaluations/grades/calculating-gpa).

If you cannot find a semester average on a transcript, that does not establish that the school never calculates one. Check the student portal, transcript legend, or registrar’s explanation.

### Academic Progress and Eligibility Checks

For personal progress tracking, use term GPA to understand a particular semester and CGPA to understand the combined result.

For a scholarship, academic-standing review, or graduation requirement, read the exact rule. Look for whether it specifies a term average, a cumulative average, a minimum number of credits, or a particular set of courses. These conditions are separate; satisfying one numerical GPA target does not prove that every requirement is met.

### College and Graduate Applications

An application may request your cumulative result even if the field simply says “GPA.” It may also ask for separate figures by institution or a specific academic subset.

Do not choose your strongest semester because its number is higher. Match the requested period and scope, then report the official value in the required format.



## Which One Do Colleges Ask For, GPA or CGPA?

**When a college asks for cumulative GPA, use the corresponding cumulative figure, even if your transcript calls it CGPA.** If the instructions request a term, major, or other specified average, use that measure instead.

There is no single reporting rule for every college and degree level. The following official examples show how the instructions can differ.

### What if the Form Only Says “GPA”?

Read the help text and the institution’s application guide. Check whether the field belongs to a particular school, degree, semester, or subject group.

If it remains unclear, ask: “My transcript reports a semester GPA and a cumulative CGPA. Which value should I enter, and should I keep the original scale?”

If your school does not calculate GPA, do not invent an official value. For instance, [STARS provides explicit options for schools that do not report cumulative GPA](https://starsrecord.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/37318196120347-International-Applicant-How-To-Enter-Cumulative-GPA-And-Class-Rank). Use the relevant application’s instructions.



## How to Calculate GPA for One Semester

For a standard credit-based calculation:

**Term GPA = Total quality points for the term ÷ total GPA credits for the term**

For each included course:

**Quality points = Grade-point value × course credits**

The [University of Illinois registrar explains this quality-points method](https://registrar.illinois.edu/courses-grades/calculate-your-gpa/). Your institution determines the grade-point values and which courses enter the calculation.

### Worked GPA Example

This is an **original hypothetical example**, using A = 4.0, B = 3.0, and C = 2.0. All listed courses count toward GPA, and there are no repeated or pass/fail courses.


| **Course** | **Grade** | **Grade-point value** | **GPA credits** | **Quality points** |
| ----------- | --------- | --------------------- | --------------- | ------------------ |
| Mathematics | A | 4.0 | 4 | 16.0 |
| English | B | 3.0 | 3 | 9.0 |
| Biology | B | 3.0 | 3 | 9.0 |
| History | C | 2.0 | 2 | 4.0 |
| **Total** |  |  | **12** | **38.0** |


Add the quality points, then divide by the included credits:

**Semester GPA = 38 ÷ 12 = 3.1666…**, or approximately **3.17** when rounded to two decimal places.

The A in four-credit Mathematics contributes more than the C in two-credit History. Simply averaging the four grade-point values would give 3.00, which would not reflect the unequal course credits.

For a fuller course-by-course walkthrough, read [how to calculate GPA](https://studentcalctools.com/blog/how-to-calculate-gpa/). To reproduce this example in the GPA calculator, enter the four letter grades and their respective credits.



## How to Calculate CGPA Across Semesters

The cumulative calculation uses the same basic approach over a larger record:

**CGPA = Total included quality points across terms ÷ total included GPA credits across terms**

When you have compatible semester GPAs and the matching credits, the equivalent formula is:

**CGPA = Sum of (semester GPA × semester GPA credits) ÷ total semester GPA credits**

The second formula is exact when the semester GPAs retain full precision and the term records combine into the same cumulative course set. Rounded term GPAs or different cumulative rules can produce a different result.

### Worked CGPA Example Using the Same Student

Keep the first semester from the previous example. Suppose the student then completes two more terms under the same grading rules.


| **Semester** | **GPA credits** | **Quality points** | **Semester GPA** |
| ------------ | --------------- | ------------------ | ---------------- |
| Semester 1 | 12 | 38.0 | 3.1666… |
| Semester 2 | 18 | 63.0 | 3.50 |
| Semester 3 | 15 | 57.0 | 3.80 |
| **Total** | **45** | **158.0** |  |


**CGPA = (38 + 63 + 57) ÷ (12 + 18 + 15)**

**CGPA = 158 ÷ 45 = 3.5111… ≈ 3.51**

The student now has two accurate but different results:

- **Latest semester GPA:** 3.80 out of 4.00.
- **CGPA through three semesters:** approximately 3.51 out of 4.00.

The current semester is stronger than the cumulative record. The 3.80 does not replace the earlier grades; it joins them in the cumulative calculation.

Use your available semester GPAs and corresponding GPA credits in the CGPA calculator. If your transcript provides exact quality-point totals, those totals are preferable for reconciling a small rounding difference with the official record.

### Why You Should Not Simply Average Semester GPAs

In this example, the semesters contain 12, 18, and 15 GPA credits. They should not have equal influence.

The simple average is:

**(3.1666… + 3.50 + 3.80) ÷ 3 ≈ 3.49**

The credit-weighted result is approximately **3.51**. Averaging the three GPAs equally assigns the 12-credit term the same weight as the 18-credit term, which is not the intended calculation.

If every semester has the same included credits, a simple average of the unrounded semester GPAs gives the same result as credit weighting. With unequal credits, use the weighted formula; any matching simple average would be a coincidence, not a reliable method.



## How to Update CGPA After a New Semester

You can combine an existing cumulative record with a new term without entering every previous course again, provided the grading basis stays consistent and no older grades need adjustment.

**New CGPA = [(previous CGPA × previous GPA credits) + (new term GPA × new GPA credits)] ÷ total GPA credits**

Suppose a student has a 3.20 CGPA over 60 GPA credits and earns a 3.80 GPA over the next 15 credits. Treat those averages as exact for this example.

**New CGPA = [(3.20 × 60) + (3.80 × 15)] ÷ 75**

**New CGPA = (192 + 57) ÷ 75 = 3.32**

The strong semester raises the cumulative average by 0.12. It does not raise the cumulative average all the way to 3.80 because the new term represents only 15 of the 75 included credits.

This gives a useful relationship:

**Change in CGPA = (new term GPA − previous CGPA) × new credits ÷ total credits**

Under these assumptions, a term above your previous CGPA raises it, a term below it lowers it, and an equal term GPA leaves it unchanged. More accumulated credits reduce the effect of a new term of the same size.

Do not add a cumulative total and then add its component semesters again. That would count the earlier coursework twice.



## Can You Convert GPA to CGPA or CGPA to GPA?

“Conversion” can mean three different tasks. Identify which one you need before using a formula.

### Combining Semester GPAs Into CGPA

This is aggregation on the same scale. Use the semester GPAs and their corresponding GPA credits, as shown above.

One semester GPA alone cannot determine a multi-semester CGPA. It does not contain the earlier results or the credits attached to them.

### Finding a Semester GPA From Cumulative Results

A single CGPA cannot reveal each term’s GPA. Different combinations of semester results can produce the same cumulative average.

However, if you know the cumulative quality points and GPA credits both before and after a term, you can isolate the term:

**Term GPA = (new cumulative quality points − previous cumulative quality points) ÷ credits added for that term**

This works only when that term is the sole change. Retakes, grade corrections, or newly posted transfer work can change the older record and invalidate that assumption.

### Converting a 10-Point CGPA to a 4.0 GPA

Changing scales is a different task from combining terms. The labels GPA and CGPA do not establish a universal conversion formula.

For example, multiplying 8.0 out of 10 by 0.4 produces 3.2 arithmetically. That does not establish that a receiving university considers those academic records equivalent.

Follow the receiving institution’s instructions. [Stanford Graduate Admissions explicitly tells applicants to keep the transcript’s original GPA scale](https://gradadmissions.stanford.edu/apply/faq). Where conversion is required, use the institution’s specified method or requested credential evaluation.

A concrete illustration is [MIT’s published 4.0 conversion method](https://registrar.mit.edu/classes-grades-evaluations/grades/calculating-gpa): it changes the values assigned to course grades and recalculates. It does not instruct students to multiply the final 5.0-scale GPA by 4/5.

The same caution applies to percentages. “CGPA × 9.5” or “CGPA × 10” is not an automatic rule for every institution. Use a formula only when the relevant institution prescribes it.



## Why Your Calculator Result May Differ From Your Transcript

A calculator can correctly average the inputs and still differ from your official result if the inputs do not match the school’s calculation rules.

### GPA Credits Are Not Always the Same as Earned Credits

Use credits that actually belong in the GPA denominator. Do not automatically use all earned credits or remove every failed course.

For example, the [Illinois registrar’s calculation includes an F-grade course with zero quality points and five GPA hours](https://registrar.illinois.edu/courses-grades/calculate-your-gpa/). Separately, [Illinois excludes credit/no-credit courses from GPA](https://registrar.illinois.edu/registration/registration-process/credit-no-credit/), even though those courses can count toward total credit hours.

Those are institutional examples. Check your own rules for failed, withdrawn, incomplete, audited, and pass/fail coursework.

### Retakes Can Change the Cumulative Course Set

Do not assume a repeated course erases the first attempt. [MIT counts both attempts in term and cumulative GPA](https://registrar.mit.edu/classes-grades-evaluations/grades/grading-policies/repeating-subject).

If your institution instead replaces or excludes an earlier attempt, recompute the affected cumulative record using that rule. Adding old semester averages without adjustments may preserve a grade that no longer belongs in the official CGPA.

### Rounded Semester GPAs Lose Precision

In the worked example, the first semester’s exact GPA is 38 ÷ 12. If you enter its rounded value, 3.17, multiplying by 12 gives 38.04 rather than the original 38 quality points.

That difference is small, but it explains why term-level estimates may not reproduce an official cumulative figure exactly. Keep available precision during calculations and follow your institution’s display rule at the end. [Illinois, for example, truncates its transcript GPA to two decimal places rather than rounding](https://registrar.illinois.edu/courses-grades/calculate-your-gpa/).

### Different Scales and Credit Systems Cannot Be Mixed Directly

Do not place a 3.50 out of 4.00 and an 8.50 out of 10.00 into the same weighted average. They are not expressed on a common grading basis.

Also check whether the credits use a consistent unit. A transfer evaluation or institutional rule may be needed before combining records. A cumulative calculator does not decide which outside courses your school accepts or how it treats their grades.



## Choose the Calculator That Matches Your Record

Start with the period you want to measure and the information you have.

Use the GPA calculator when you have individual course grades and credits on its supported 4.0 scale. Use the CGPA calculator when you have compatible semester GPAs and matching GPA credits to combine.

For official reporting, return to your transcript and the application instructions. The right number has the right period, scale, and coursework behind it.
