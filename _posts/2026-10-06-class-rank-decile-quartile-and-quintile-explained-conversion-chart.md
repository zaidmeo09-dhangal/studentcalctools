**A class rank decile divides students into 10 groups, a quintile into 5 groups, and a quartile into 4 groups. Each group represents about 10%, 20%, or 25% of the class, respectively.**

If your school uses the top-down numbering shown in this guide, decile 1 means the highest 10%, quintile 1 means the highest 20%, and quartile 1 means the highest 25%.

These groups describe a range of positions. They do not reveal your exact rank.

If you already know your rank and graduating class size, use the Class Rank Calculator to [find your percentile and standing](https://studentcalctools.com/class-rank-calculator/). Then use the conversion chart below to understand how that standing fits into different class rank brackets.



## **Why Do Schools Use Class Rank Brackets?**

Brackets let a school describe a student’s general academic standing without reporting an individual position such as 18th out of 300.

Reporting practices vary. A school might provide:

- An exact rank and class size.
- A decile, quintile, or quartile.
- A percentile or top percentage.
- A school profile showing the class’s GPA distribution.
- No individual class rank.

For example, [Upper Merion Area High School’s class rank policy](https://hs.umasd.org/info/procedures/class-rank-policy) says that individual ranks are not recorded on student records. Its school profile instead includes weighted GPA ranges for each decile. The policy also allows an institution to request an exact rank when required for admission or scholarships.

A GPA distribution on a school profile is therefore not necessarily the same thing as an official decile assigned to you.

Check your transcript, school profile, or counselor’s explanation before interpreting a bracket.



## **What Does Each Class Rank Bracket Mean?**

This guide numbers groups from the highest academic standing downward: group 1 is the highest group. Confirm that your school uses the same convention.


| Reporting system | Number of groups | Approximate share in each group | Highest group |
| ---------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Decile | 10 | 10% | Decile 1: top 10% |
| Quintile | 5 | 20% | Quintile 1: top 20% |
| Quartile | 4 | 25% | Quartile 1: top 25% |


### **What Is a Class Rank Decile?**

A class rank decile places you in one of ten groups.

Under the top-down convention:

- Decile 1 covers the highest 10%.
- Decile 2 covers the next 10%: outside the top 10%, but within the top 20%.
- Decile 3 covers the next 10%: outside the top 20%, but within the top 30%.
- The pattern continues through decile 10.

In a class of 300 students with distinct ranks and equal-sized groups, decile 1 contains ranks 1–30. Decile 2 contains ranks 31–60.

“Second decile” does not mean second place. It describes a group.

### **What Is a Class Rank Quintile?**

A quintile divides the class into five groups of approximately 20%.

In a class of 300 students with distinct ranks:


| Quintile | Rank range |
| -------- | ---------- |
| 1 | 1–60 |
| 2 | 61–120 |
| 3 | 121–180 |
| 4 | 181–240 |
| 5 | 241–300 |


The first quintile includes the first two deciles under this grouping method.

### **What Is a Class Rank Quartile?**

A quartile divides the class into four groups of approximately 25%.

In a class of 200 students with distinct ranks:


| Quartile | Rank range |
| -------- | ---------- |
| 1 | 1–50 |
| 2 | 51–100 |
| 3 | 101–150 |
| 4 | 151–200 |


The first quartile means the highest quarter of the class under the convention used here.

Quartile and quintile are different. The first quartile includes the highest 25%; the first quintile includes the highest 20%.

That is why a decile does not always convert into one exact quartile.



## **How to Calculate Your Class Rank Decile**

If you know your exact rank and class size, start with your top percentage:

**Top percentage = (Your rank ÷ Class size) × 100**

Here:

- Your rank is your position, with 1 representing the highest rank.
- Class size is the number of students included in your school’s ranking.

For a simple top-down bracket estimate, use:


| Bracket | Calculation |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Decile | (Your rank ÷ Class size) × 10, then round upward |
| Quintile | (Your rank ÷ Class size) × 5, then round upward |
| Quartile | (Your rank ÷ Class size) × 4, then round upward |


Round upward to the next whole number. If the result is already a whole number, leave it unchanged. For example, 2.01 becomes 3, while 2.00 stays 2.

These calculations estimate brackets using rank share. Your school’s official method takes priority.

### **Example: Rank 23 Out of 244**

First, calculate the top percentage:

Top percentage = (23 ÷ 244) × 100 = approximately 9.43%

Then calculate the decile:

Decile calculation = (23 ÷ 244) × 10 = approximately 0.943

Round upward to 1.

Under this method, the student is in decile 1 because their rank falls within the highest 10%.

### **What Happens at a Cutoff?**

Using the same class of 244:


| Rank | Top percentage, rounded for display | Estimated decile |
| ---- | ----------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| 24 | 9.84% | 1 |
| 25 | 10.25% | 2 |


Use the unrounded calculation to decide which side of the cutoff a rank falls on. Rounding the displayed percentage should not move someone into another bracket.

Schools may handle uneven group sizes or tied ranks differently, so confirm official placement with your counselor.



## **How to Calculate Your Quintile or Quartile**

The process is the same, but the number of groups changes.

### **Quintile Example**

Suppose you rank 60th in a class of 300:

Quintile calculation = (60 ÷ 300) × 5 = 1

That places you in quintile 1 under this method.

At rank 61:

Quintile calculation = (61 ÷ 300) × 5 = approximately 1.017

Round upward to 2. Rank 61 falls in quintile 2.

### **Quartile Example**

Suppose you rank 50th in a class of 200:

Quartile calculation = (50 ÷ 200) × 4 = 1

That places you in quartile 1.

At rank 51:

Quartile calculation = (51 ÷ 200) × 4 = 1.02

Round upward to 2. Rank 51 falls in quartile 2.



## **How Do Class Rank Brackets Map to Percentile?**

Brackets count groups from the top in this guide. Percentiles describe standing in the opposite direction: a higher percentile generally means a stronger position.

However, an exact conversion depends on the percentile formula.

The StudentCalcTools calculator uses:

**Inclusive percentile = [(Class size − Your rank + 1) ÷ Class size] × 100**

The “+ 1” includes your own position.

For a student ranked 31st out of 300:

Inclusive percentile = [(300 − 31 + 1) ÷ 300] × 100 = 90

Their top percentage is:

Top percentage = (31 ÷ 300) × 100 = approximately 10.33%

This student has a 90th-percentile estimate under the inclusive formula but falls just outside the top 10% by rank share.

That is why you should not use a rounded percentile alone to decide whether you meet an exact top-percentage cutoff.

For more examples, see [percentile vs percentage vs top %](https://studentcalctools.com/blog/class-rank-percentile-vs-percentage/).



## **Can You Convert a Bracket Into an Exact Rank?**

No. A bracket gives you a range, not a single position.

If a school reports that you are in the first quartile of a 200-student class, the equal-group example places you somewhere between ranks 1 and 50. It does not tell you which rank is yours.

The same limitation applies when converting between bracket systems:

- First quintile can mean decile 1 or decile 2.
- First quartile can include decile 1, decile 2, or part of decile 3.
- Third decile can fall partly in quartile 1 and partly in quartile 2.

You need more detailed information to make a narrower claim.

A calculator also cannot recover your exact rank from GPA alone. The result depends on your classmates’ GPAs and your school’s ranking rules.



## **What Do Colleges See When Your School Reports by Decile?**

When a school officially reports a decile, the college receives a range of academic standing rather than an individual rank.

The school profile can help explain that range. [Common App’s guidance on school profiles](https://www.commonapp.org/files/developing-a-school-profile.pdf) identifies grading policies, class rank, and GPA distribution as useful context for understanding an applicant’s academic record.

Depending on what your school provides, that context may include:

- The GPA scale.
- Whether the school weights advanced courses.
- How class rank is reported.
- GPA ranges within the graduating class.
- Available courses and curriculum.

A first-decile label compares you with students in your school’s ranked group. It does not mean you are in the highest 10% of all U.S. students.

When reporting academic information, follow the application’s instructions and your official school records. For example, [STARS instructs students to report rank as it appears on their transcript](https://starsrecord.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/37153702602907-How-To-Enter-Your-Cumulative-GPA-And-Class-Rank), rather than converting it into another format.

If you are trying to understand your standing in relation to your goals, read [what’s a good class rank percentile](https://studentcalctools.com/blog/what-is-a-good-class-rank/).



## **Common Mistakes When Reading Class Rank Brackets**

### **Confusing a Group With an Exact Position**

Decile 1 does not mean rank 1. In an evenly divided class of 300, it covers 30 positions.

### **Treating Quartile and Quintile as the Same**

A quartile represents about 25% of the class. A quintile represents about 20%.

### **Assuming Every School Numbers Groups the Same Way**

This article uses group 1 for the highest academic standing. Check the definitions in your school’s own documents.

### **Using a Rounded Percentile to Claim a Cutoff**

A displayed 90th percentile is not sufficient evidence of an official top-10% placement. Check the exact rank, class size, and school policy.

### **Assuming a GPA Guarantees a Certain Decile**

There is no universal GPA requirement for the first decile. A 3.8 GPA can represent different positions in different graduating classes.

### **Ignoring Ties or Small Class Sizes**

Groups may not contain exactly equal numbers of students. Ties and small cohorts can make the school’s grouping method especially important.
