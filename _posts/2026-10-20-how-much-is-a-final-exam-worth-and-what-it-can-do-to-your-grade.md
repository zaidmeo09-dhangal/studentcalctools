  How much is a final exam worth?

  It depends on your school, district or teacher, and the number is in your
  syllabus. Real examples include 1/7 (about 14.3%) in Fort Worth ISD, 10% in
  Era ISD, 20% in Plano ISD high schools and 30% for Florida's state
  end-of-course tests.

  ---

  How much can a final exam drop my grade?

  Your grade changes by the weight times the gap between your exam score and
  your current grade. With a 90, a 20% final and a 60 on the exam, you drop to
  84. A 0 would drop you to 72.

  ---

  Is 20% a lot for a final exam?

  It counts as one fifth of the course, so it is large enough to matter. With a
  90 going in, a 20% final can move you anywhere from 72 to 92, but a 10-point
  miss costs only 2 points.

  ---

  If I have a 90 and the final is worth 20%, what do I need to keep an A?

  You need a 90 on the final to finish with exactly a 90. The calculation is (90
  − 90 × 0.80) ÷ 0.20 = 90. If your A starts at 93, a 20% final cannot get you
  there from a 90.

  ---

  What happens if I skip or fail the final?

  A skipped final usually counts as a zero. With an 85 and a 20% final, a zero
  leaves you at 85 × 0.80 = 68. Failing with a 50 leaves you at 78. Check your
  syllabus for exemptions or a rule that you must pass the exam.

  ---

  How much are final exams worth in college?

  There is no standard number, because most instructors set it in the syllabus.
  Some schools set limits, like the University of Ottawa, which requires a final
  to count between 30% and 60% unless the Faculty authorizes otherwise.

  ---

  Can a final exam raise my grade a full letter?

  Only if you are close to the next cutoff. At a 20% weight, a 100 adds at most
  0.2 × (100 − your grade) points. From an 88 that is 2.4 points, enough to
  reach a 90. From an 82 it is 3.6 points, which is not enough.

  ---

  How do I find my final exam weight?

  Check your course syllabus first, then your online gradebook or district
  grading policy. If you still cannot find it, ask your teacher how much the
  final counts toward the course grade.

  ---

  What if my class uses points instead of percentages?

  Divide the final's points by the total points in the course to get its weight.
  A 200-point final in a 1,000-point course is worth 20%, and you can use that
  number in any of the formulas above.

**New course grade = current grade × (1 − final exam weight) + final exam score × final exam weight.** Use the weight as a decimal, so 20% becomes 0.20. In plain words, your grade moves toward your exam score by the weight of the exam.

There is no single answer to how much a final exam is worth. Your state, district, school or teacher sets the weight, and the real number is in your syllabus or grading policy. In the sourced examples below it runs from about 10% to 30% for school courses, and one university's rule allows anything from 30% to 60%. If you already know your weight and want a target score, the ++[final grade calculator](https://studentcalctools.com/final-grade-calculator/)++ does the work for you. The rest of this guide shows the math, so you can see what a final can really do to your grade.



## **Key takeaways**

- A final exam has no national weight. Your syllabus or district grading policy has the real number.
- The formula is new grade = current grade × (1 − weight) + exam score × weight.
- Shortcut: your grade changes by weight × (exam score − current grade). Score above your current grade and you go up, score below it and you go down.
- With a 90 going in and a 20% final, the best you can finish with is a 92 and the worst is a 72.
- The higher your current grade, the more a final can take away and the less it can add.
- If the score you need comes out above 100, that target is out of reach through the final alone.



## **How much is a final exam worth?**

It depends on where you study. Some states set the weight of certain finals by law, some districts set it for every high school course, and in college the instructor sets it in the syllabus. Here are real examples with the original sources:


| **Where** | **Final exam weight** | **Source** |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fort Worth ISD, Texas | 1/7 of the semester grade (about 14.3%) | ++[2024-25 secondary guide](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1743630242/fwisdorg/b6fql6x3q1tg00nquaqq/SecondaryGuidetoGradeReporting20242025830242.pdf)++ |
| Garland ISD, Texas | 1/7 of the semester average when an exam is given | ++[District regulation](https://garlandisd.net/media/16309/download)++ |
| Era ISD, Texas | 10% of the semester grade | ++[Grading guidelines](https://files-backend.assets.thrillshare.com/documents/asset/uploaded_file/5992/Eisd/f69b67e8-053d-412a-b288-650bb8dbd88c/26-27-Era-ISD-Grading-Guidelines.docx-%281%29-%281%29.pdf?disposition=inline)++ |
| Plano ISD, Texas | 20% of the semester grade in grades 9 to 12, and 10% in grades 7 and 8 | ++[Board policy EIAA (LOCAL)](https://pisd.diligent.community/document/9b2105f5-1b2e-4de3-96fd-b43b30d5d9cb/)++ |
| Florida (state law) | 30% for the state end-of-course test in Algebra 1, Geometry, Biology, Civics and U.S. History | ++[Florida Statutes 1008.22](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/statutes/2020/1008.22)++ and a ++[Miami-Dade guide](https://pdfs.dadeschools.net/ESE/fsaa/EOCFSAAFlyerWithoutDate.pdf)++ |
| Georgia (state board rule) | 20% for end-of-course tests, and since 2023 districts may lower it to 10% | ++[Dade County Schools](https://www.dadecountyschools.org/departments-programs/testing/ga-milestones-assessment)++ and an ++[Augusta Press report](https://theaugustapress.com/state-department-of-education-to-lower-weight-of-georgia-milestones-tests)++ |
| University of Ottawa, Canada | Between 30% and 60% of the course grade unless the Faculty authorizes otherwise | ++[Academic Regulation A-8](https://www.uottawa.ca/about-us/leadership-governance/policies-regulations/a-8-evaluation-student-learning)++ |


Two notes on that table. Florida's end-of-course test is a state exam, and ++[Pasco County Schools says it does not replace the course's own semester or final exam](https://eschool.pasco.k12.fl.us/?p=15536)++. Georgia's rule has changed before, so check the current weight with your school.

College is different again. Most colleges let each instructor choose the weight, and some set limits, like the University of Ottawa rule above. That is why two courses in the same department can weight their finals very differently, and why the syllabus is the only number you can trust.

Is there a "best" weight? One paper on course design looked at the math and found that the grade you need on the exam to reach the next letter stops getting easier as the weight climbs past about 30%. The author concluded that a final worth 10% to 30% leaves the most room for student work to show up in the grade       (++[Franke, 2018, Teaching & Learning Inquiry](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1174864.pdf)++). That is one author's analysis, not a rule your school has to follow.



## **The final exam grade formula**

Here is the formula again, with each part explained:

**New course grade = current grade × (1 − final weight) + final exam score × final weight**

- **Current grade** is your grade before the final, for all the other work in the course.
- **Final weight** is the share of the course the final is worth, as a decimal. A 25% final is 0.25.
- **Final exam score** is the percentage you get on the exam.

If you do not know your current grade yet, work it out first with the ++[grade calculator](https://studentcalctools.com/grade-calculator/)++, then use that number here.

There is also a shortcut that makes the math easier to picture:

**Change in your grade = final weight × (exam score − current grade)**

So your grade moves toward your exam score, and the weight tells you how far. At 20%, it moves one fifth of the way. If you have an 85 and score a 95 on a 20% final, you move one fifth of the 10 points between them, so you finish at 87.

The same idea works for the Texas semester exam. In Fort Worth ISD the three six weeks each count 2/7 and the exam counts 1/7, so the exam pulls your semester grade one seventh of the way toward its score. With a 90 average across the six weeks and a 70 on the exam, you get 90 × 6/7 + 70 × 1/7 = 87.1. A 100 would lift you to 91.4 and a 0 would drop you to 77.1.

## **What can a 10%, 20%, 25% or 30% final do to your grade?**

The table shows the lowest and highest course grade you could finish with. The low number is what happens if you score 0 on the final. The high number is what happens if you score 100.


| **Current grade** | **10% final** | **20% final** | **25% final** | **30% final** |
| ----------------- | ------------- | ------------- | ------------- | ------------- |
| 70 | 63.0 to 73.0 | 56.0 to 76.0 | 52.5 to 77.5 | 49.0 to 79.0 |
| 80 | 72.0 to 82.0 | 64.0 to 84.0 | 60.0 to 85.0 | 56.0 to 86.0 |
| 90 | 81.0 to 91.0 | 72.0 to 92.0 | 67.5 to 92.5 | 63.0 to 93.0 |


A few things stand out:

- **A high grade has more to lose than to gain.** At 20%, a student with a 90 can gain at most 2 points but can lose 18. A student with a 70 can gain up to 6.
- **Weight changes the stakes, not the direction.** A 30% final can swing the same student by 30 points between a 0 and a 100. A 10% final swings it by 10.
- **A bad day costs less than it feels like.** A final that lands 20 points below your current grade costs 2 points at 10%, 4 points at 20%, 5 points at 25% and 6 points at 30%.

## **Worked example: "I have a 90 and the final is worth 20%"**

This is one of the most common questions students ask, so here it is step by step. Your current grade is 90 and the final is worth 20%.

1. Multiply your current grade by the share that is already graded: 90 × 0.80 = 72.
2. Add the exam's share: exam score × 0.20.
3. Add the two parts together.

Here is what different exam scores do:


| **Score on the final** | **Your course grade** |
| ---------------------- | --------------------- |
| 100 | 92.0 |
| 95 | 91.0 |
| 90 | 90.0 |
| 85 | 89.0 |
| 80 | 88.0 |
| 70 | 86.0 |
| 60 | 84.0 |
| 0 | 72.0 |


Notice that a 90 on the final keeps you at a 90. Anything higher than your current grade pulls you up, and anything lower pulls you down.

### **What score do you need for a target grade?**

Rearrange the formula to find the score you need:

**Score needed = (target grade − current grade × (1 − weight)) ÷ weight**

With a 90 and a 20% final:


| **Target course grade** | **Score needed on the final** |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| 92 | 100 |
| 90 | 90 |
| 88 | 80 |
| 85 | 65 |
| 80 | 40 |
| 70 | Already secured, since even a 0 gives you a 72 |


If your school starts an A at 93, a 90 with a 20% final tops out at 92, so that A is out of reach unless there is extra credit.

### **Two more quick examples**

- **A 78 with a 25% final, aiming for an 80.** Score needed = (80 − 78 × 0.75) ÷ 0.25 = 86. You need an 86 or better.
- **An 82 with a 25% final, aiming for a 90.** Score needed = (90 − 82 × 0.75) ÷ 0.25 = 114. That is more than 100, so a 90 is not possible through the final alone.



## **The best case and worst case rule**

Before you study, work out two numbers. They tell you how much the final can really change your grade.

- **Worst case:** current grade × (1 − weight). This is what you finish with if you score 0.
- **Best case:** worst case + 100 × weight. This is what you finish with if you score 100.

If the worst case is still above the grade you want, you have already secured it. If the best case is below the grade you want, you cannot reach it with the final. If your target sits between the two, the final decides it, and the score-needed formula tells you how high you have to go.

Since the best case depends so much on where you start, a final only lifts you a full letter if you are already close to the cutoff. At a 20% weight, a 100 adds at most 0.2 × (100 − your grade) points. From an 88 that is 2.4 points, enough to cross 90. From an 82 it is 3.6 points, which is not enough to reach 90.

## **What if your class uses points instead of percentages?**

Turn the final into a percentage first. Divide the points the final is worth by the total points in the course.

For example, a 200-point final in a 1,000-point course is worth 200 ÷ 1,000 = 20%. After that, use the same formulas. Your current grade is the percentage you have earned on the points graded so far, as long as every non-final item is already in the gradebook. If other assignments are still ungraded, your current grade is only an estimate.



## **How to find your final exam weight**

1. Open your course syllabus. Most list the final exam weight next to the other grade categories.
2. Check your online gradebook or portal. Some show category weights.
3. Read your district or school grading policy. Many districts publish one, and the examples above show what to look for.
4. Ask your teacher three questions: how much the final counts, whether it can replace a lower grade, and whether you have to pass the exam itself to pass the course.

## **Common mistakes to avoid**

- **Including the final in your current grade.** Your current grade should cover everything except the final, or the math double counts it.
- **Typing the weight in the wrong form.** In the formula, 20% is 0.20. In the calculator, you enter 20.
- **Assuming a strong final erases a weak semester.** At 20%, even a perfect score moves you only one fifth of the way up.
- **Skipping the final because the math looks safe.** A missed exam usually counts as a zero, and that is the worst case in the table above. Check your syllabus first, since some courses also require you to pass the exam itself.



## **Final thoughts**

A final exam is rarely the whole story, but it is rarely small either. Find the weight in your syllabus, work out your best and worst case, and then you know how much studying time it deserves.
