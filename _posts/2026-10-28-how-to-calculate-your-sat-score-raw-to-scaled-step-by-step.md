**To calculate your SAT score, count the questions you got right in each section, convert each count to a scaled section score from 200 to 800, then add the two section scores for a total from 400 to 1600. On the real digital SAT, the exact conversion depends on question difficulty and which second module you received, so College Board's own practice guides give a range instead of one fixed number.**

This guide shows how SAT scoring works, how to convert raw scores using College Board's official practice test tables, and why the same number of correct answers can lead to different scores. Every figure comes from College Board documents, and where College Board does not publish an exact number, the article says so.



## **Key takeaways**

- Each section is scored from 200 to 800 in 10-point steps, and the total is the sum of the two sections, from 400 to 1600.
- A raw score is the number of questions you answered correctly. Wrong answers are not subtracted.
- The digital SAT has 98 questions, and 90 of them are scored. The other 8 are unscored pretest questions.
- The same raw score can convert to different scaled scores on different test forms.
- Practice test guides from College Board give a score range, because the home method is a simplified version of the real one.



## **How is the SAT scored?**

The SAT has two sections, Reading and Writing and Math, and each section has two modules. College Board's [Assessment Framework for the Digital SAT Suite](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/assessment-framework-for-digital-sat-suite.pdf) describes the scoring this way:

- **Each question is scored 1 or 0.** A multiple-choice question scores 1 if your answer matches the key and 0 otherwise. For Math grid-in questions, the system cleans up your entry and compares it with the keyed answers.
- **The test uses item response theory.** Your score depends on whether you answered correctly, on attributes of the questions such as difficulty, and on whether your pattern of answers suggests guessing.
- **Module 2 adapts.** Module 1 has a broad mix of easy, medium and hard questions. Based on how you do, you get a second module that is, on average, harder or easier.
- **Section scores are added.** The total score is the arithmetic sum of the Reading and Writing score and the Math score.

Here is how the questions break down:


|  |  |  |  |  |
| ------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------- | -------------------- | ----------------- |
| **Section** | **Questions** | **Scored** | **Unscored pretest** | **Section score** |
| Reading and Writing | 54 (two modules of 27) | 50 | 4 | 200 to 800 |
| Math | 44 (two modules of 22) | 40 | 4 | 200 to 800 |
| Total | 98 | 90 | 8 | 400 to 1600 |


The unscored pretest questions look the same as the others, so you cannot tell which ones they are.

## **What is a raw score?**

Your raw score is the number of questions you answered correctly. It is the starting point for every conversion, but it is not your SAT score.

- A correct answer scores 1.
- A wrong answer scores 0, the same as a blank. Nothing is subtracted.
- On the real digital test, your count out of 54 or 44 can include a few unscored questions you could not identify, which is one more reason a home calculation can only estimate the official score.

Because a blank scores the same as a wrong answer, it costs nothing to answer every question.



## **How the 200 to 800 section scores work**

Section scores run from 200 to 800 in steps of 10. The total score runs from 400 to 1600, also in steps of 10. College Board kept these familiar scales when it moved the test online, so a total score reads the same way it always has.

Section scores add up in simple ways. A 1400 total can come from any pair that sums to 1400:


|  |  |  |
| ----------------------- | -------- | --------- |
| **Reading and Writing** | **Math** | **Total** |
| 700 | 700 | 1400 |
| 650 | 750 | 1400 |
| 750 | 650 | 1400 |
| 600 | 800 | 1400 |




## **Why the same raw score can scale differently**

Two things move the conversion around.

**1. Your second module.** On each section, module 1 is a broad mix of question difficulty. The cut between students who get the harder module 2 and those who get the easier one is set around the median section score, so roughly half of test takers go each way. Many middle scores are reachable from either path, and College Board's simulations found that deliberately missing questions in module 1 to get the easier module 2 would likely lower a student's score. Its conclusion is that students are best served by answering every question to the best of their ability.

**2. The test form.** Each test has different questions. College Board says two students can get the same number correct on two different forms and still receive different reported scores.

![Diagram of SAT module 1 routing students to a harder or easier module 2](/assets/images/adaptive-module-routing-diagram.webp)

College Board's own practice test guides show the second effect clearly. These guides are for the paper versions of its digital practice tests. They use a simplified method and report a range. Here are the same raw scores on two of them:

Same number of correct answers, different ranges. Each practice test has its own table, so never use one test's table for another test.

## **Reading and Writing: raw score to scaled score**

On College Board's paper practice tests, Reading and Writing has 66 questions, split into two modules of 33. You add the correct answers from both modules and look up the total. Here are sample rows from the [Practice Test #4 guide](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/scoring-sat-practice-test-4-digital.pdf):


|  |  |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| **Reading and Writing raw score** | **Section score range** |
| 20 | 370 to 390 |
| 30 | 450 to 470 |
| 40 | 530 to 550 |
| 50 | 610 to 630 |
| 60 | 700 to 720 |
| 66 | 790 to 800 |


The real digital test is shorter, with 54 Reading and Writing questions, so do not apply these rows to a real score report.



## **Math: raw score to scaled score**

The paper practice Math section has 54 questions, split into two modules of 27. Here are sample rows from the same guide:


|  |  |
| ------------------ | ----------------------- |
| **Math raw score** | **Section score range** |
| 20 | 360 to 390 |
| 30 | 470 to 500 |
| 40 | 590 to 620 |
| 46 | 690 to 720 |
| 50 | 750 to 780 |
| 54 | 790 to 800 |


A perfect Math raw score of 54 reaches 790 to 800, while 54 out of 66 in Reading and Writing is only 640 to 660. That is because the two sections have different lengths on this paper test.



## **Worked example: from correct answers to a total score range**

Here is a full walkthrough using the Practice Test #4 tables. The student's correct answers are made up for the example.


|  |  |  |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------- | ---------- |
| **Step** | **Reading and Writing** | **Math** |
| 1. Correct in module 1 | 27 of 33 | 22 of 27 |
| 2. Correct in module 2 | 24 of 33 | 20 of 27 |
| 3. Add the modules for the raw score | 51 of 66 | 42 of 54 |
| 4. Look up the section range in the table | 620 to 640 | 620 to 650 |


Then add the lower values and the upper values separately:

- Lower total: 620 + 620 = 1240
- Upper total: 640 + 650 = 1290

So the practice score range is **1240 to 1290**. The same raw scores on the Practice Test #7 tables give 600 to 660 in each section, a total range of 1200 to 1320. That is the form effect from the table above.

On the real digital SAT you do not do this lookup yourself. College Board scores the test and your report shows both section scores and the total. If the report shows Reading and Writing 640 and Math 650, your total is 1290.



## **Why your score changes between test dates**

A new test date usually means a new form, with different questions and a different mix of difficulty. College Board's scoring accounts for question difficulty, which is why the same number of correct answers does not always give the same score. The adaptive second module adds another source of difference, since students get questions targeted to their performance in module 1.

Expect small differences between practice tests and the real thing for the same reason.



## **How to estimate your digital SAT score**

1. **Take a full-length practice test in Bluebook.** College Board offers free adaptive practice tests in the same app used on test day, so it is the closest preview of the real test.
2. **Use the right guide for paper practice tests.** If you took a paper practice test, use that test's own scoring guide, and expect a range.
3. **Use calculators for planning only.** No calculator can reproduce College Board's method exactly, since even College Board's practice guides call their own method simplified.
4. **Check the official score report.** After the real test, the report shows the section scores and the total.



## **Common mistakes to avoid**

- **Treating a paper practice table as the real conversion.** The real digital test has fewer questions and an adaptive second module.
- **Counting all 98 questions as scored.** Only 90 count.
- **Expecting wrong answers to cost points.** They score 0, the same as a blank.
- **Comparing raw scores across different tests.** Each form has its own conversion.
- **Trying to game module 1.** College Board's simulations say it lowers the likely score.



## **Final thoughts**

SAT scoring comes down to a simple idea: correct answers become section scores, and section scores add up to the total. The details, such as question difficulty and the adaptive module, are why a single chart can only estimate.
