**A good SAT score in 2026 depends on your goal, but 1200 is a strong score nationally (75th percentile of SAT users), 1350 is competitive for many selective colleges (89th percentile) and 1500 is elite (97th percentile). The national average for the class of 2026 is 1045.**

A good SAT score is one that fits the colleges and scholarships you are aiming for, not one magic number. This guide gives you the official national average, College Board's percentile table, and real score ranges from colleges that publish them, so you can set a target that makes sense for you. ## **Key takeaways**

- The mean SAT score for the class of 2026 is 1045, up 16 points from the class of 2025, according to College Board.
- A 1200 is the 75th percentile of recent SAT users, a 1400 is the 93rd and a 1500 is the 97th.
- The 50th percentile of SAT users sits at 1020, so a score above that is above the middle of the test takers.
- Colleges publish their own middle 50% ranges, and those ranges matter more than a national label.
- Test policies change by college and by year, so check the current policy before you plan.



## **What is the average SAT score in 2026?**

College Board's 2026 SAT Suite annual report says [the mean total score for the class of 2026 was 1045](https://newsroom.collegeboard.org/sat-turns-100-nearly-2-million-students-class-2026-take-test), a 16-point increase over the class of 2025. It was the second year in a row of gains.


| **Class of 2026** | **Mean score** |
| ------------------- | -------------- |
| Total | 1045 |
| Reading and Writing | 528 |
| Math | 517 |


College Board also reports that 42% of class of 2026 test takers met or exceeded both its Reading and Writing and Math college and career readiness benchmarks. Those benchmarks are 480 for Reading and Writing and 530 for Math, which adds up to 1010.

An average is not the same as a good score. The mean of 1045 falls between the 53rd and 55th user percentiles, which means it is close to the middle. Most students applying to selective colleges need more than that.



## **SAT score bands and percentiles**

A percentile tells you how your score compares with other test takers. College Board publishes two kinds in its [SAT percentile tables](https://research.collegeboard.org/reports/sat-suite/understanding-scores/sat):

- **User group percentiles** are based on the actual SAT scores of students who graduated in the past three school years.
- **Nationally representative percentiles** come from a research study of U.S. 11th and 12th graders and are weighted to represent all students in those grades, whether or not they usually take the SAT.

The user percentile is the more useful number for college planning, because it compares you with actual SAT takers. Here are the key benchmarks:


| **Total score** | **User percentile** | **Nationally representative percentile** |
| --------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| 1000 | 47th | 48th |
| 1100 | 62nd | 67th |
| 1200 | 75th | 81st |
| 1300 | 85th | 91st |
| 1350 | 89th | 94th |
| 1400 | 93rd | 97th |
| 1500 | 97th | 99th |
| 1550 | 99th | 99+ |


And here is how the common score bands compare:


| **Score band** | **User percentile range** | **How to read it** |
| -------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Below 1000 | Below 47th | Below the middle of recent SAT users |
| 1000 to 1190 | 47th to 74th | Around the middle to above average |
| 1200 to 1349 | 75th to 88th | Top quarter of SAT users |
| 1350 to 1499 | 89th to 97th | Top 11% and higher |
| 1500 to 1600 | 97th to 99th+ | Top 3% and higher |

## **What is a good SAT score by college goal?**

There is no official admissions cutoff for "good," so the best benchmark is what colleges report about their own students. Many colleges publish the 25th and 75th percentile scores of enrolled first-year students in their Common Data Set, in section C9. Here are examples from colleges that publish a range, plus the test policy each one stated in that data:


| **Goal** | **Reference point** | **How it compares nationally** |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| College readiness | College Board benchmarks of 480 in Reading and Writing and 530 in Math, or 1010 combined | A baseline for success in college courses, not an admissions target. 1010 is the 48th user percentile |
| Broad-access public university | Texas State University: middle 50% of 990 to 1200 for enrolled students who sent SAT scores (Common Data Set 2024-25). Test scores are optional for most applicants | 990 is the 45th user percentile and 1200 is the 75th |
| Flagship public university | Texas A&M University: middle 50% of 1160 to 1390, with a median of 1280, for fall 2025 enrolled students who sent SAT scores. Test optional | 1160 is the 70th user percentile, 1280 the 83rd and 1390 the 92nd |
| Ivy League | Harvard College: middle 50% of 720 to 770 in Reading and Writing and 730 to 790 in Math for the fall 2025 entering class (Common Data Set 2025-26). Harvard requires SAT or ACT scores again | Section scores of 720 and 770 in Reading and Writing are the 95th and 99th user percentiles. Math scores of 730 and 790 are the 94th and 99th |


Three things to keep in mind when you read a table like this:

- **Ranges describe enrolled students, not minimums.** A score below the 25th percentile does not rule you out, and a score inside the range does not guarantee admission.
- **Only students who sent scores are counted.** At test-optional colleges, the published range leaves out students who chose not to send one, so it can skew higher than the whole class.
- **Policies change.** Harvard reinstated its testing requirement for students applying to enter in fall 2025, and other colleges have changed policies in recent years too. Check the current rules on the college's admissions page.

If a college you like does not appear in its Common Data Set with a score range, be careful with ranges you see online. They are often estimates from third parties and can disagree with each other.

## **What do section scores say?**

Colleges also look at section scores, and percentiles for them differ from total score percentiles. College Board's tables give these user percentiles:


| **Section score** | **Reading and Writing** | **Math** |
| ----------------- | ----------------------- | -------- |
| 600 | 72nd | 75th |
| 650 | 84th | 84th |
| 700 | 92nd | 91st |
| 750 | 98th | 95th |


A balanced score, such as 650 and 650, adds up to a 1300, which is the 85th percentile overall.



## **How to set your target score**

1. **List the colleges you are considering.** Include a range, from likely to reach.
2. **Find each college's published range.** Search for the college's Common Data Set, then look at section C9 for the 25th, 50th and 75th percentile SAT scores. The [Common Data Set initiative](https://commondataset.org/) explains what each section reports.
3. **Check the testing policy for your application year.** Some colleges require scores, some make them optional and some change from year to year.
4. **Compare your goal with the percentiles above.** A score near a college's 75th percentile puts you in the stronger part of its range. A score near the 25th percentile is still within range, but at the lower edge.
5. **Find your gap.** Subtract your current score from your target. If you are at 1180 and want a 1280, that is 100 points, or about 50 per section.
6. **Plan how you will practice.** If you want to know how raw answers turn into scores, read how to calculate your SAT score before you start tracking progress.

The link in step 6 goes to our guide on [how to calculate your SAT score](https://studentcalctools.com/blog/how-to-calculate-sat-score/).



## **Common mistakes to avoid**

- **Treating the average as a target.** The average is the middle of all test takers, not a standard for college.
- **Mixing up percentile types.** A 1400 is the 93rd user percentile but the 97th nationally representative percentile, so check which one a source is using.
- **Trusting unsourced ranges.** Third-party sites often disagree. Use each college's own data when you can.
- **Forgetting that policies change.** A test-optional college this year might require scores next year, or the reverse.
- **Treating a score as a guarantee.** Test scores are one part of an application, along with grades, course rigor and more.
